That matters if you track policy for a living. Lobbyists, government-affairs teams, advocacy groups, local reporters and consultants all need to know what was *said*, not just what passed.

## Why minutes aren't enough

Most local bodies are required to keep **minutes**, and many publish **"action minutes"**: who attended, what motions were made and how each member voted. The debate, public comment, staff answers and a council member's offhand commitment usually aren't in them. Minutes are also adopted at the *next* meeting, so they can trail the meeting by two weeks or more.

State legislatures vary. Some publish floor journals and committee reports, but verbatim committee transcripts are rare, and they're slow when they do exist. Meanwhile, most legislatures and many cities now stream to **YouTube** or a video portal such as **Granicus** or **Swagit**, with agendas and minutes in a system like **Legistar**. The video is usually the only complete record.

## Accuracy and quoting

Auto-captions struggle with names, acronyms and crosstalk at the dais. Treat the transcript as a **working copy**: verify any quote against the video at its timestamp before it goes in a newsletter or a story, and cite the minutes or journal for official actions. The same advice applies to federal hearings; see our [congressional hearing transcript guide](/congressional-hearing-transcript).

Outside the US the approach is the same. UK, Canadian and Australian councils widely webcast meetings, and many post them to YouTube.

## FAQ

**Do city councils publish verbatim meeting transcripts?**
Most don't. Councils are usually required to keep minutes, which typically record motions, votes and attendance rather than every word spoken. The meeting video is usually the only complete record, so a transcript of the video is the fastest way to get the full discussion as text.

**How do I get a transcript of a state legislature committee hearing?**
Find the hearing video on the legislature's YouTube channel or video portal, copy the link, and paste it into YTTranscript.app. You get the full caption text with timestamps in seconds, as long as the video has captions.

**Is an auto-caption transcript an official record?**
No. It's a working copy for research and finding quotes. Before quoting someone publicly, check the line against the video at the timestamp, and cite the official minutes or journal for votes and actions.

**What if the meeting is on Granicus, Swagit or another portal instead of YouTube?**
Some portals show their own captions or a text index, but downloading them is often clunky. Many governments also post the same meeting to YouTube, which you can transcribe in seconds. If there's no YouTube copy, use the portal's captions or a speech-to-text tool on the recording.
