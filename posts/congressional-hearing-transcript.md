**The fastest free way to get a congressional hearing transcript is to pull the auto-captions from the hearing's YouTube video, which is usually possible within hours of the hearing ending.** The official transcript, published by the Government Publishing Office on GovInfo and Congress.gov, is the authoritative record, but it often takes months to appear and some hearings are never printed at all. If you need a verbatim transcript within the hour, that is what paid services like **CQ Transcripts** and **Bloomberg Government** sell.

This guide compares every option, free and paid, then walks through the same-day workflow.

## Why official hearing transcripts take so long

A published hearing is more than a transcript. The printed record bundles the testimony with prepared statements and material submitted before and after the hearing, and each committee decides whether and when to publish it. The Congressional Research Service puts it plainly: printed hearings often aren't published for months, although witness testimony is usually online much sooner.

So on the day of a hearing, the "official transcript" people search for usually doesn't exist yet. What does exist is video, and video has captions.

## How to get a same-day transcript from YouTube

**Step 1: Find the hearing video.** House committees stream their hearings to committee-run YouTube channels, and Congress.gov's committee video pages collect many of them. Senate committees webcast on their own websites and Congress.gov instead, but high-profile Senate hearings are often streamed in full on YouTube by outlets such as PBS NewsHour.

**Step 2: Give the replay time to caption.** Captions on a finished livestream aren't instant. YouTube processes the replay first, which typically takes a few hours and can stretch to a day for very long sessions. ## Can you quote an auto-caption transcript?

For news, research and internal briefings, yes, with one rule: **verify every quote against the video before you publish it.** Auto-captions stumble on names, acronyms and crosstalk, and hearings have plenty of all three. C-SPAN's clickable caption text is a quick second check for hearings it aired. Cite the video itself, not the transcript tool; see [how to cite a YouTube video](/how-to-cite-a-youtube-video).

For court filings, legislative histories and anything that needs the authoritative record, wait for the official printed hearing on GovInfo, or use a verbatim service in the meantime.

## State legislatures and city councils

The same workflow works for state legislatures, county boards and city councils that stream on YouTube, and it's often more valuable there. Official minutes usually summarize the actions taken rather than record every word, so the caption transcript may be the only searchable record of what was actually said. ## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take for an official congressional hearing transcript to be published?**
Often months, and sometimes years. The Congressional Research Service notes that printed hearings often are not published for months, and each committee decides whether and when to publish, so some hearings are never printed. Witness statements usually appear online much sooner.

**Where can I find a congressional hearing transcript for free?**
Once published, official hearing transcripts are free on GovInfo and Congress.gov. For recent hearings, the fastest free option is the auto-caption transcript of the hearing's YouTube video, and C-SPAN's Video Library shows searchable caption text for the programs it airs.

**Does C-SPAN provide transcripts of congressional hearings?**
Not as documents. C-SPAN's Video Library uses closed-caption text to make programs searchable, and clicking a line jumps the video to that moment, but C-SPAN says it does not provide or sell copies of transcripts and suggests contacting the committee.

**What is the fastest way to get a verbatim hearing transcript?**
A paid service. CQ Transcripts says it delivers transcripts of every congressional hearing within 60 minutes, and Bloomberg Government provides AI summaries within an hour and a human-reviewed verbatim transcript within 24 hours. Both are sold as organizational subscriptions.

**Are Senate hearings on YouTube?**
Usually not on official committee channels: Senate committees webcast on their own websites and Congress.gov. But high-profile Senate hearings are often streamed in full on YouTube by outlets such as PBS NewsHour, while House committees stream their hearings to committee-run YouTube channels.
